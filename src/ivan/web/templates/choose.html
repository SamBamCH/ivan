The slate chooser picks every contest at once. Each candidate is a (contest, lineup) pair carrying its net dollars (fee × ROI) in every simulated world, and the worlds are the slate's shared draw, so world w is the same game in every contest. Each step adds the pair that most raises the slate's log return, mean over worlds of log(1 + net / bankroll), with the bankroll in dollars. With a bankroll well above the fees the log is nearly linear and the picks sit close to the shrunk mean-ROI picks; a smaller bankroll figure asks for more caution. The per-contest tiles above are this contest's share of the book; the trace's "gain" is the utility each pick added to the slate.

{% elif v.method == 'portfolio' %}

The portfolio chooser judges the book, not the lineup. Every candidate carries its ROI in each simulated world; each step adds the lineup that most raises the book's log return summed over worlds, with the bankroll as the scale. A lineup that wins in worlds the book already wins adds little, so the picks spread across outcomes, and a lineup that wins two of two thousand worlds no longer outranks one that cashes steadily. The chart below still shows quality (cash % and ROI) for reference; the trace's "gain" is the utility each pick added.

{% else %}

The chooser scores every candidate on quality (a weighted blend of its simulated cash % and ROI, each scaled 0–1 across the candidates), takes the best one, and then repeatedly takes the lineup with the highest quality + λ × distance from the lineups already picked. Distance is measured between the lineups' simulated point totals across all simulations: two lineups that boom and bust together are close, even if they share few players.

How the numbers behind the picks are made

A world is one way the slate could go: a draw of every game's score (the team-points latents the player model is built on). The sim draws {{ v.book.chosen.worlds if v.book and v.book.chosen else 'a thousand' }} of them, and every contest on the slate sees the same worlds.

Inside a world, a lineup's score is still uncertain, because its players have their own game-to-game noise, but that uncertainty is a plain bell curve with a known centre and width. The field is the sum of such curves, one per candidate lineup, each weighted by how many copies of it the field is expected to hold. So for every score the lineup could post, the sim knows how many entries it would expect ahead, how many of those are lumps of duplicated chalk, and what the ladder pays at that rank once its own copies split the prize. The cell is that payout averaged over the lineup's bell curve: "in this world, this lineup is positioned for this much money."

That is the same expectation a counted simulation would converge to, with the sampling noise integrated out (Rao–Blackwell). A lineup is no longer rewarded for the one world in two thousand where its single draw happened to finish first; it is rewarded in every world where it was genuinely close, and a chalk lineup that shares its top finishes with hundreds of copies is charged for them. Row means are expectations, so the picks are chosen on positioning, not on luck, and the honest mean ROI tile above should sit close to the in-sample one.

Everything downstream is unchanged: the rows are stacked into the same lineup × world matrix, the greedy or portfolio method reads it exactly as before, and the distance between lineups is still measured on their per-world traces.
